# Hoshin Kanri — strategy deployment, and the last mile

> Review of *Understanding Hoshin Kanri* — the Lean Enterprise Institute's 18-page compilation of five Lean Post articles (Womack, Reich, Greco, Mottola ×2), filed 2026-09-27 on the vault-level shelf; provenance, source URL, checksum and the file itself are on its document page ([[2026-09-27-hoshin-lei-ebook]]). What the method is, what the five articles actually contain, and the part that matters one level down from the board room: an objective dies on the front line unless the level receiving it is given a number, a date, a name and a method. Related: [[supervisor-operating-rhythm]], [[how-open-work-is-managed]]

## What Hoshin Kanri is, in the publisher's own words

- The ebook's definition is the Lean Lexicon's: hoshin kanri (strategy or policy deployment) is "a management process that aligns — both vertically and horizontally — an organization's functions and activities with its strategic objectives", carried by "a specific plan — typically annual — ... with precise goals, actions, timelines, responsibilities, and measures." Nothing in the ebook goes further than that definition with a template.
- The cascade has two directions: objectives go down, the *how* comes back up. A strategic objective needs a **companion operational objective at the value-creating level**, or it is a wish — in Womack's plant example, the strategic objective (survive an industry-level disruption) required a plant objective (raise the rate, cut cost, hold quality, spend no capital) because the strategic one needed the money it would free.
- **The failure the method exists to prevent is in the last mile, not in the vision:** "What starts with the loud voice of the CEO at the top becomes a faint whisper by the time it reaches the organization's front lines", because front-line managers are "mostly absorbed with daily chaos, with no workable method for addressing hoshin objectives even when they are clearly stated and prioritized for that level."
- The skeleton-and-muscle model (Reich): the plan holds the organization together, daily improvement moves it; unused muscle atrophies, and a muscle that is not attached to the skeleton produces no purposeful movement. Misalignment is manufactured upstream, by design: when executives are measured on their function's own goals rather than the organization's, priorities conflict whatever the cascade says.
- Where the problems come from: the gemba, not the planning room — the shop floor surfaces the obstacles that block the real value-added work, which is why the walk is the input to the plan rather than a check on it.

## The one operational mechanism in the ebook

- A school district's turnaround (Greco) is the only article that describes a working cadence, and its shape transfers: **45-day learning cycles**, cascaded balanced scorecards at every level, and **leader rounding twice a year with a fixed four-question protocol** — what is working well; what barriers are in your way and do you have the resources you need; what ideas do you have; who has been helpful that I can thank on your behalf — run as listening, explicitly not as evaluation.
- Its routing rule for the size of a problem is worth copying: solution clear → just do it; solution unknown but the problem simple → PDSA; complex, many people, solution unknown → a chartered project. Published routing is what stops a two-hour fix being escalated into a project, and the reverse.
- And its budget discipline, which is a resource rule before it is a culture rule: "for the first few years, we stopped buying stuff, stopped chasing isolated efforts" — the money went into capability, and the dollars saved were reinvested in people.

## The coaching half (two of the five articles)

- Mottola's two parts: transformation is personal growth first — reflection, self-awareness, humility — and what people resist is the unknown rather than change itself, which is why "hoshin kanri demystifies such uncertainty by providing a structured way to walk the path while it is being co-created."
- Her five areas: learn from the previous planning process instead of discrediting it; make it personal through practice, because people follow leaders they see walking the talk; build trust through facts rather than blame, leaving motive out of it ("go hard on the system, but soft on people"); balance dissatisfaction with inspiration, since a case built only on what is wrong goes stale; and coach at every level, because the deployment is passed on by learning by doing.
- Honest value: strong orientation for anyone who has to lead a change, and no cadence, template or measurement of any kind — the author says as much by calling them "intangible truths".

## How the five articles grade

- **Provenance is strong.** LEI publishes it and the vocabulary is the Lean Lexicon's, so the definitions can be cited as the originating institute's.
- **Method density is low.** Of the five, one observes a cascade inside a plant, one is a model essay (skeleton and muscle), two are a coaching essay in two parts, and one is a school-district case study — two of the five are not about manufacturing at all, and none contains an X-matrix, a catchball protocol, an annual calendar or a worked front-line example.
- **It is also a lead-generation asset**: the closing pages are LEI's course catalogue, which is worth knowing before quoting it as doctrine.
- Verdict: read it for the shape of a cascade and for the exact failure mode it names; do not treat it as a procedure. The two pieces a front-line manager actually needs are outside it — a routine with enough protected time to work an objective at all ([[supervisor-operating-rhythm]]) and a loop that stops an objective sliding into a wish ([[how-open-work-is-managed]]).

## What it settles for the last mile

- An objective arriving at the front line has to become four things the same day, or it decays into a whisper: a **number** (what is different, by how much), a **date**, a **named owner**, and a **method** (the experiment loop or the loss drill that will move it).
- The precondition is stability, not willpower. Toyota's cascade landed in a plant running better than 98% of the time with about a thousand andon pulls a day answered inside takt — which is precisely why its managers were "not bogged down in fire-fighting" and had time to think about hoshin targets at all. A front line without that stability cannot absorb an objective; the objective then competes with the chaos it was meant to resolve.
- Deploy the objective *through* the front-line leaders, not past them. In the plant Womack walked, the redesign of jobs and equipment was done by a group leader with four team leaders and the operators themselves over three months, against a stated objective — not handed to staff experts. A target assigned over the heads of the people who own the process arrives as a number with no method.
- Do not bolt a second cadence on to carry it. The objective should ride the routine that already exists, because "a standing rhythm that keeps firing while changing nothing" is the documented failure ([[supervisor-operating-rhythm]]).
- A number that arrives as a cascade target is the place to be most careful about Goodhart: pair it with a counter-measure, and read the trend before it is allowed to become a judged score ([[supervisor-operating-rhythm]] carries the pairing rule).

## Open

- Where the annual objectives actually come from in this employer, who cascades them, and what the front-line version of one looks like: not documented, and not something this ebook supplies. It is a question to ask upward rather than a page to invent.
