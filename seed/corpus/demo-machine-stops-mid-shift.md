# A machine stops mid-shift — what order to check in

## First thing: hold your position, not the machine

New people often rush straight in to fix the machine. The first thing is to pin down the state: which mode the machine is stopped in, whether it is holding a half-finished part in the mould or chamber, and who is near the machine. Only once you are sure the machine is in a safe state may you go near it.

## Four questions in order, no skipping

1. Is there an error displayed? Copy the exact code or wording, do not interpret it. The error message is the only thing the machine says for itself.
2. When exactly did it stop? Compared with the line's takt — a stop right after a changeover is usually a setting problem; a random stop is usually material, heat, or a sensor.
3. Where did it stop? One station, one axis, or the whole line? A single point is the machine; the whole line is material or power.
4. What changed just before? A shift handover, a new batch of material, a new operator, a maintenance action — the most recent change is the first suspect.

## If you have not found it in a few minutes: escalate, do not keep trying

The right way to handle a stop is time-boxed escalation: the operator handles it for a short window; past that, call the team leader; past the next window, call maintenance. When everyone knows their threshold, nobody stands watching the machine die. Conversely, one person fixing it alone for too long is the most expensive way to keep a machine down.

## After it runs again: do not skip the last step

Write the symptom and the way it was handled into the notebook, with a photo if needed. Do not record the plant's output figures; record only the symptom, the check order, and what actually solved it. Next time it happens, the person on shift can read it and follow it, without calling anyone.
