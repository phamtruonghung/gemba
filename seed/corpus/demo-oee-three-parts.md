# OEE has three parts — use it right and do not overuse it

## Three parts, three different questions

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality, but the meaning is in three questions:

- Availability: is the machine ready to run when it is needed? Loss here is machine stops, waiting for material, waiting for people.
- Performance: when it runs, does it run at its designed rate? Loss here is slow running, small stops, constant in-and-out.
- Quality: is what it makes usable straight away? Loss here is scrap, rework, rerunning a batch.

These three parts are only useful when they are *kept separate* in the report. A single combined number cannot say what to do; the three parts point straight to who (maintenance, production, quality) has to act.

## Two common mistakes

The first is comparing OEE between two different plants or two different lines. A different definition of "planned time" means every number behind it is different. Compare it only with itself, on one definition, over time.

The second is using OEE as a competition score. Then people fix how downtime is recorded, not why the machine stops. OEE is a pointer for finding where losses are, not a scoreboard for people.

## How to start on one line

Pick one line, write the definition of each part on one sheet posted at the spot, and record downtime by cause before calculating any number. Measure causes correctly for a few weeks before computing the index — otherwise you only produce a good-looking number for a presentation.

## When not to use it

On a line with a very short takt and many stations in sequence, the person at the machine needs hourly TAED (target, actual, efficiency, downtime) more than a daily OEE figure. Choose the metric by the job the person receiving it has to do now.
