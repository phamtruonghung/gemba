# FMDS: the daily management board

> Distilled from the Art of Lean reference library (artoflean.com): its FMDS learning guide and the Gemba Coach entry on what FMDS stands for. The guide credits its structure to a Japanese-language book by former Toyota supervisors at OJT Solutions, and it is careful to say the four-level row format is a simplification used at some overseas plants, not the one Toyota layout. Related: [How open work is managed](/notes/vault-how-open-work-is-managed) · [Hoshin Kanri — strategy deployment, and the last mile](/notes/vault-hoshin-kanri-strategy-deployment-and-the-last-mile)

## What it is

FMDS — the Floor Management Development System — is a daily-management structure that connects company policy to what a work group does today, and carries the result back up. In Japan it has the plainer name 日常管理板, "daily management board": how you manage the day. Manage the day, every day, against the standard.

It runs on a board. Each work group has one, the group leader runs it, and it hangs in a high-traffic place on the floor — not in an office, not on a screen.

The longer English name earns its length: it is not a metrics board with a different label. It is a system for **obtaining results while developing the people** who get them. The chain is the point — the board develops people, the people carry out the activities, the activities move the metrics, and problem solving runs through all of it.

## The structure: columns and levels

Across the top, the columns are the hoshin categories — typically safety, quality, productivity, cost, and people development. Each column descends from the outcome the company needs to the daily work the group does about it.

Inside a column, two levels do the work:

- **The management point** — the outcome trend the group watches to know whether policy is being met. A monthly count of a defect type, for instance.
- **The inspection point** — the cause trend the group watches to know whether its improvement work is having an effect. The daily count of the equipment-trouble cases behind those defects, for instance.

The management point watches the result; the inspection point watches the lever. The worked example in the source is worth keeping because it shows the whole loop in miniature: a group's workplace policy was "zero defects by a deadline"; investigation showed packaging defects and foreign-object contamination were the common types, so they became the management point; further investigation showed equipment trouble was the cause, so equipment-trouble cases became the inspection point. The group tried one countermeasure for a week, saw little movement, switched, saw the inspection point fall and the defects follow, and kept adjusting while recording the trend.

Some plants — typically newer to the method — formalise the rows further, into four named levels: a main indicator tied to hoshin, a sub-indicator broken down to countermeasure level with spikes annotated, the activity itself with evidence of PDCA, and an activity result with a logical connection back to the sub-indicator. Each level carries explicit standards: target stated, abnormality marked, responsible person named. Experienced groups work with fewer formal levels; the principle — outcome, cause, action, result — is the same, and the prescription scales with the site's maturity.

## The two daily cycles

The board is not read once and left alone. Two cycles run on it, together:

1. **Manage the indicators.** The person in charge records the day's data **by hand** — not printed, not typed elsewhere and pasted. The group updates the management and inspection points, checks them against the target line, and sees whether today is normal or abnormal. On track: the standard holds. Off: the gap is visible without analysis.
2. **Work the problems the first cycle surfaces.** When the indicators show a gap, the group investigates the cause, develops a countermeasure, carries it out, and checks whether the inspection point moved. This is not a separate activity; it is what the board exists to drive.

Two kinds of target line make normal and abnormal visible at a glance. A **sloping** line is used when the goal is to reduce something over time — bring a defect count toward zero by a date — and each day's point is read against where the line says you should be by now. A **horizontal** line is used when the goal is to hold a value at or below a level every day. One watches a trend, the other watches a level; both make the gap obvious without an explanation.

Hand-plotting is deliberate, not a relic. Writing the number forces the person to look at it, notice what changed, and take ownership of what they are recording.

## Two structures that keep it honest

- **Focus activity.** When the indicators surface a gap that needs sustained work rather than a quick fix, the group leader opens a focus activity: problem defined, cause analysed, countermeasure planned and executed, result checked — displayed on the board so the work does not disappear between shifts or meetings.
- **Change point management.** When something changes in the group's work, the leader documents what changed, what the risk is, who is responsible for managing it, and when the change condition ends. The four things to scan are the classic four: man, method, material, machine. Changes are where defects, safety incidents and instability most often start, and writing them down is what stops a surprise from becoming a problem.

## Developing people through it

The progression the group leader goes through is short enough to remember: **see the problem** (learn to read the board and recognise abnormal), **show the problem** (make it visible to others and teach the team members to see what the leader sees), **solve the problem** (connect floor problem solving to plant targets). It is built over years of daily use, not in a course.

Team members get the same treatment. Standardised work, kaizen and problem solving are learned in training; the board is where they are expected to use those skills on their own group's real gaps. When the group leader hands a specific problem to a team member, that person investigates, proposes and follows through, and the board records who is working what and whether the result moved.

Managers above the group leader are the forgotten audience. People who have never worked with a board do not know how to interact with it — what to look for, what to ask, how to coach rather than check numbers. Sites new to the method have run dedicated practice environments for exactly this.

## How it fails

- Treating the board as a results scoreboard. The numbers are the vehicle, not the purpose.
- Taking it off the floor — digitised, projected in a meeting room, or replaced by a dashboard nobody stands in front of. The board's value is partly that people walk past it and start talking.
- Making it look good for visitors instead of showing the real current state.
- Installing boards without developing the group leaders who run them.

## What to check before trusting a board

- Does a number trace up to a company aim **and** down to a daily activity? If not, it is a list, not a management point.
- Is there an inspection point under each management point — a cause trend, not another result?
- Is the data written in a person's hand, on the floor, by the person responsible?
- When a gap appears, is there a name, a countermeasure and a date attached — and a check that says whether it worked?
- Are changes being recorded as changes, or discovered as problems afterwards?

## Where it does not reach

- The board develops people; it does not replace the operating rhythm of meetings, notes and decisions that already exists — [The supervisor's operating rhythm](/notes/vault-the-supervisor-s-operating-rhythm-the-craft).
- It carries a group's gaps; it does not set company policy. The cascade it hangs off is [Hoshin Kanri](/notes/vault-hoshin-kanri-strategy-deployment-and-the-last-mile).
