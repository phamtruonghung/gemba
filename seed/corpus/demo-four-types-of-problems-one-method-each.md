# Four types of problems, one method each

> Distilled from the Art of Lean reference library (artoflean.com), from its guide to the four types of problems. That guide is honest about its own provenance and this note keeps the honesty: the four-type taxonomy is the author's organising model, published by the Lean Enterprise Institute, and it is **not** official Toyota terminology — Toyota does not number its problems one through four. What travels to any floor is the routing, not the brand. Related: [How open work is managed](/notes/vault-how-open-work-is-managed) · [The supervisor's operating rhythm](/notes/vault-the-supervisor-s-operating-rhythm-the-craft)

## The failure the model is aimed at

Most problem-solving training teaches one method and implies it fits everything. On a floor the hard part usually comes earlier: recognising what kind of problem is in front of you. A machine that just stopped, a defect that keeps coming back, a cost target that needs a better process, and a product that does not exist yet are four different problems, and they need four different habits of thought. Solving all four with one tool is the most expensive mistake in the discipline — it either runs a deep investigation on something that needed a quick restore, or it "fixes" a recurring defect with a symptom patch that guarantees it returns.

The dividing line is the **standard**. Performance below the standard is a recovery problem. Performance above it, or a standard that should not exist as it is, is an improvement problem.

## The four types, and what each asks for

| Type | The situation | What the work is | A method that fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — troubleshoot | Something has gone wrong now: a stop, a bad batch, a late shipment | Restore function fast and safely, without making it worse | The four C's: concern, cause, countermeasure, check |
| 2 — gap from standard | A standard exists and performance fell short of it, repeatedly | Eliminate the cause so the problem stops recurring | Toyota's eight-step method; 8D in a supply base |
| 3 — target condition | Nothing is "wrong"; today's method is simply wasteful | Raise the standard: shorter lead time, better flow, better built-in quality | The six-step kaizen cycle |
| 4 — open-ended | No defined problem at the start; new value, new product, new process | Create something that does not exist yet | Design thinking, TRIZ, lead-user work — no single canonical sequence |

Two are reactive (1 and 2 restore performance) and two are creative (3 and 4 raise or create a standard). A group that can only do 1 firefights; a group that can only do 2 investigates a stopped machine for a week; a group that only does 3 keeps improving a process that is quietly broken; a site that does no 4 is competent and eventually irrelevant.

## What each type looks like when it is done well

**Type 1 is a craft, not a shrug.** The four C's are deliberately light, because the value of troubleshoot is speed. Restore the abnormality, note the immediate cause, name what you did, confirm it held — then hand the recurrence question to Type 2. A plant that pulls an andon many times a day and resolves each within seconds or minutes by the team member and team leader closest to the work is running Type 1 as a system, not as improvisation. Its limit is its definition: it treats the symptom, so on its own it becomes a firefighting cycle.

**Type 2 is the deepest and most used.** The eight steps are: clarify the problem, break it down, set a target, analyse the root cause, develop countermeasures, see them through, evaluate both results and process, then standardise and share. 8D, practical problem solving and the older five- and six-step Toyota routines order and name the steps differently and share the same spine: define, analyse, find the cause, counter it, confirm, standardise.

**Type 3 assumes the current method can be beaten.** It asks "how should this ideally work?" rather than "why did performance fall?", which makes it divergent first and convergent later — a different mental posture from root-cause analysis, and the reason a team that is good at Type 2 does not automatically do Type 3 well.

**Type 4 is the only one that creates new business.** It expects failure as part of learning. It is usually associated with product development, but any function can be innovated — the same nuisance that began as a Type 1 stop and became a Type 2 root-cause case has driven Type 4 leaps on machining lines: new sensing, a different washing method, a redesigned machine architecture that changed the problem entirely.

## Using it at the shift meeting

- Ask the question the routing depends on, every time a problem is raised: **which type is this?** It is the question that decides how much of the supervisor's week the problem is entitled to.
- Route explicitly. A stop is Type 1, handled on the spot, recorded. A recurrence of the same stop is Type 2 and gets a named owner, a target and a review date. "This line could be laid out better" is Type 3 and belongs in the improvement plan, not the shift log. "Should we be making this part at all" is Type 4 and belongs above the group leader.
- Watch for a type pretending to be another. A Type 2 problem logged over and over as Type 1 is a defect the group has decided to live with. A Type 3 wish written into the Type 2 queue will never be closed, because there is no standard to be back inside of.
- The types are lenses, not boxes: one problem can move up through several over its life. What must not happen is applying one type to everything.

## What this settles for the library

- The method follows the type. Writing the type down before the method is what stops a two-hour fix being escalated into a project, and the reverse.
- Every version of structured problem solving in circulation — 6, 8, 12 steps, DMAIC, 8D — is a descendant of the same scientific loop: observe, define, hypothesise, test, check, learn. Arguing about the step count is arguing about granularity.
- A group that wants to raise its game usually needs the type it avoids, not more training in the one it likes.

## Where it does not reach

- The published taxonomy is one author's model, and its own guide says so. Do not attribute the four numbers to Toyota in a report or a training deck; attribute the practice of routing by type, which is what a floor can actually copy.
- Type 4 needs a mandate and resources no group leader holds. Recognising it is the group leader's job; authorising it is not.
