# Job Relations, the four steps

> Distilled from the Art of Lean reference library (artoflean.com): its Job Relations learning guide — the four steps and the worked case that opens it. Job Relations is the TWI method for handling a problem with a person; like Job Instruction it is deliberately short enough to fit on a card a supervisor carries. Related: [Job Instruction, the four steps](/notes/demo-job-instruction-the-four-steps) · [The supervisor's operating rhythm](/notes/vault-the-supervisor-s-operating-rhythm-the-craft)

## The case the method exists to prevent

A supervisor has a worker who has been reliable for months. One day the worker does not come in. The supervisor remembers an older pattern — a habit of missing the same day of the week — decides the pay envelope has made it possible again, and meets the worker the next morning with a punishment: go home for the week and think about it.

The worker had been told by a neighbour, who also works at the plant, to pass on word that he had been called away: his father had been hurt. The neighbour forgot. Nobody asked.

Judge the supervisor's action on three counts and it fails all three: how the person feels about him now, how the rest of the team feels about how the person was treated, and what it did to production. He had one fact — the absence — and a stale memory, and from those he built a story confident enough to act on.

That is the most natural mistake in supervision: not cruelty, but **a confident story built on one fact.**

## The four steps

| Step | What the supervisor actually does |
|---|---|
| 1 — Get the facts | Review the record (the whole of it, not the part remembered); find out what rules and customs apply; talk with the person; get opinions and feelings; be sure you have the whole story |
| 2 — Weigh and decide | Fit the facts together and look for gaps and contradictions; consider what they bear on each other; list the possible actions; check policy and practice; weigh each action against the individual, the group and production; do not jump to conclusions |
| 3 — Take action | Decide whether to handle it yourself, get help, or refer it up; watch the timing; do not pass the buck |
| 4 — Check results | Decide how soon and how often to check; watch output, attitudes and relationships; did the action actually help? |

The order is not a suggestion. Facts come before deciding, because you cannot weigh facts you do not have. Deciding comes before acting, because acting first is just reacting to the first move that comes to mind. Acting is always followed by checking, because an action nobody checks is an action nobody learns from.

## The two ideas that carry the method

**Opinions and feelings are facts.** What a person thinks — whether or not it is correct — is real to that person and shapes what they do. A supervisor who collected only the paperwork has not finished step 1. These are the hardest facts to get, because people do not hand them over on request; you get them by listening, not by arguing, interrupting or doing all the talking.

**Your objective is the measuring stick.** Before weighing any action, be clear about what you are trying to achieve. In the case above the objective was simple — the person at work regularly — and against that objective, sending him home for a week makes no sense at all. The same test applies to the choice: what does this action do to the individual, to the group, and to production? All three, every time.

## What it looks like in a normal week

- The method is not only for disciplinary moments. "A problem" here means anything the supervisor has to take action on with a person: a missed handover, a dispute over who does which task, a complaint about a rota, a skill concern.
- Step 1 is the step most often shortchanged under pressure. One deliberate conversation before acting is usually the whole cost of getting it right.
- Deciding whether to refer up is part of step 3, not a failure. Knowing where your authority ends is part of knowing what action to take; passing off a problem that is yours is the failure.
- Step 4 is where a supervisor gets better. A supervisor who acts and never looks back never finds out whether the action was right, and so does not improve. Watching for a change in **attitudes and relationships**, not only output, is what catches an action that fixed the number and damaged the team.
- A firm action built on a bad read is worse than a mild one. The strength of the action is not the measure; the fit to the facts and to the objective is.

## What this settles for the library

- Handling a person badly has three costs at once — the person, the group, the work — and the corpus should name all three whenever a people problem is discussed. A supervisor who only counts the third is measuring the cheapest one.
- The four steps are a checklist, not a personality. The card exists so that a supervisor under pressure does not skip the first step, which is exactly the step pressure removes.
- Written rules and unwritten custom both govern. "Nobody told me" is often true and still not the whole story: some of what decides a case is what the area has always done.

## Where it does not reach

- This is the method for handling a problem, not for changing terms of employment, pay or discipline policy. Those are decided elsewhere and referred up.
- It assumes the supervisor has the authority to act on what they find. Where the finding requires a decision above the group leader, the honest sequence is: get the facts, then refer with the facts in hand.
