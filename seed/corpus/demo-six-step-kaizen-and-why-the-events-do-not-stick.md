# Six-step kaizen, and why the events do not stick

> Distilled from the Art of Lean reference library (artoflean.com): its Kaizen learning guide (the six-step procedure) and its Gemba Coach entry on why kaizen fails. Craft only, no plant. Related: [Four types of problems, one method each](/notes/demo-four-types-of-problems-one-method-each) · [How open work is managed](/notes/vault-how-open-work-is-managed)

## Kaizen is a loop, not an event

Telling people to "do kaizen" without giving them a procedure produces activity without results. The procedure is what turns an eye for waste into a confirmed better method: observation into analysis, analysis into ideas, ideas into a plan, plan into action, action into a standard that the next cycle starts from.

The six steps:

1. **Discover the improvement potential** — what you will try to improve, and why. Without a target and a standard to compare against, nobody can tell afterwards whether it worked.
2. **Analyse the current situation** — how the work is actually done now, from facts observed at the workplace with real parts at the real time. Not from memory, not from what the standard document says.
3. **Generate original ideas** — as many as possible before judging any of them. Judging while generating is the most common way to kill the best answer early.
4. **Develop the implementation plan** — feasibility, cost, difficulty, likely result, named responsibilities and dates. Prefer the work-method idea over the equipment idea: the improvement that needs no capital is the one worth finding first.
5. **Implement the plan** — involving the people affected, not notifying them: explain why, ask for their input, arrange the retraining.
6. **Evaluate the new method** — compare against the target from step 1, check daily at first, watch for side effects, standardise what worked. Then step 6 leads back to step 1, because a confirmed improvement immediately reveals the next thing above it.

Two things separate kaizen from problem solving even though the logic underneath is identical. Problem solving starts from a deviation: a standard exists, performance fell, close the gap. Kaizen often starts **before** any standard is violated — from a leader's eye for waste, or a challenge to improve even while today's performance is acceptable. The trigger differs; the discipline is the same.

## The step count is a clue, not a doctrine

Six, eight and twelve steps are all in use, and they all map onto the same four phases. Plan absorbs most of the steps in every version; Do is one or two; Check and Act are the confirmation and the new baseline. The count varies with the domain — kaizen on a work method, on flow, on equipment each need different analytical sub-steps. A factory running equipment improvement teaches twelve because restoration and horizontal replication matter enough to name explicitly; a factory working on work methods teaches six.

So when a version arrives from elsewhere, map its steps into Plan-Do-Check-Act and the logic appears. What the local step count does tell you is what the organisation believes the hard part is.

## Why kaizen programmes fail

The specific answer always depends on the site, but the general pattern is consistent: **organisations never make the transition from running improvement activities to building an operating and management system that holds results daily.**

- The event is not the problem — a focused effort on a defined area can produce real change. What happens after it is the problem. A week of activity cannot change permanent conditions on its own; conditions change when the gain is built into a standard and held by a routine that runs daily, with accountability and people development behind it.
- The pattern has a name in the literature — improvement bursts with no lasting effect beyond the burst. The countermeasure is not a better event; it is connecting the local change to a flow and to a management system that keeps it.
- The second failure mode is artifacts without substance: charts nobody follows, maps that live in binders, reports written and never reviewed. The documents exist; the change does not, because the people producing them lacked the domain depth to solve the actual problem.
- The third is not being able to name the problem. A problem is a gap from a standard; if no standard is clear, no problem is visible, and the improvement list fills with action items that have no bearing on cause and effect.

What the companies that sustain improvement share is a critical mass of people with both the thinking and the technical knowledge, developed by practice on the floor rather than by workshops, who define problems precisely and use standardised work as the link between daily management and daily improvement.

The Toyota way of framing it, from its own glossary, carries two conditions worth keeping verbatim: in manual work, **work-method improvement takes priority over equipment improvement**; and kaizen is not the job of specific people — every employee can and should do it, each from their own position.

## What to check before calling something kaizen

- Is there a target and a baseline, in numbers, from step 1? Without both, step 6 is an opinion.
- Was the current situation observed at the workplace, or reconstructed from the meeting room? Step 2 done from memory contaminates everything after it.
- Did the idea generation happen before the evaluation? A single idea that was obviously going to be chosen is a decision, not a kaizen.
- Is the new method written into the standard, and does the standard name who follows it and when it is checked? Standardisation without the next cycle is stagnation; improvement without standardisation evaporates.
- Has the change been given to the people who do the work, with the reason? The best plan fails if the people doing the work were not part of it.

## Where it does not reach

- The procedure is a cycle, not a schedule of events. A site that only runs events has a calendar, not a kaizen system.
- Equipment kaizen and flow kaizen need the deeper technical content their own twelve-step forms carry. The six-step cycle is the team-leader-level version for work methods; it is not a licence to redesign a machine.
