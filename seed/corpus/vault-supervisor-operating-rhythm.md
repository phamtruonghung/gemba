# The supervisor's operating rhythm — the craft

> What a supervisor's daily, weekly, monthly and quarterly routine is actually for, what the numbers layer and the people layer demand of it, and the ways it decays — read on 2026-09-26 from about thirty sources (a university business school, the Lean Enterprise Institute, Harvard Business Review, method owners such as FranklinCovey and the EOS/Scaling Up world, consultancies, and practitioner blogs). The portable half of that reading is here; every URL and its verbatim quote is in the raw source above, where vendor content and opinion-only blogs are marked as such. One employer's application of this page — which parts it runs, which it refuses, and why — lives in that employer's own scope and links here. Related: [[how-open-work-is-managed]], [[tpm-pillars-and-losses]], [[lessons]] — the annual/strategic layer above this cadence is [[hoshin-kanri]]

## The cadence and its purposes

- **Four rhythms, four purposes, and the failure is one meeting trying to be all four.** A short daily check-in (five to fifteen minutes), a weekly tactical meeting that covers only tactical issues, a monthly strategic session, and a quarterly session that is the only one allowed to change the targets — the weekly exists precisely to end the clarifying that happens in corridors afterwards ("Sneaker Time").
- **Authority, not agenda length, separates the monthly from the quarterly.** The monthly corrects inside decisions that are already fixed; the quarterly can move the targets. A quarterly review with no decisions in it is expensive theatre, and a monthly review with no corrections was a dashboard with chairs.
- **Daily management is a bundle, not a board.** The short tier meetings, leader standard work, gemba walks and process confirmation work together or not at all; a board without the walks and the leader's own routine is a display.
- **Reflection belongs at milestones, not on a calendar.** The reflection meeting (hansei) is held at key milestones and at the end of a project, and its output is countermeasures told to everyone else so the same mistake is not repeated — a scheduled "lessons" slot with nothing behind it produces nothing.
- **Every ten minutes is information or accountability, depending on the agenda.** Reviewing yesterday's numbers generates information; working open issues and assigning resources to close them generates accountability. Pick which one the meeting is.
- **Protect floor time as the default, and write the escalation triggers down.** The observed failure is a supervisor spending most of the day in offices, email and firefighting; the design goal is the inverse, with numeric triggers (a stop longer than X escalates within the shift) published rather than judged case by case.
- **Contrarian, and the reason any of this stays small:** a standing rhythm keeps firing while changing nothing when nobody owns its design — "from a distance it looks like governance; up close it is furniture" — and risk that is not managed upward is transferred downward onto people who cannot resolve it.

## The numbers layer

- **Pair every indicator with a counter-indicator** (Grove's rule in *High Output Management*): indicators direct behaviour, so each one needs a partner measure that catches the over-reaction to its extreme.
- **Five to seven, no more, each rolling up into the level above** — beyond that the few that matter stop getting attention — and **one in, one out**: a measure added retires a measure, because a number is not "key" while sitting beside thirty peers.
- **Lead measures are the ones you can move this week**; a declining leading indicator demands investigation now, not observation until the lagging number catches up.
- **Log the loss where it happens, with a reason code.** End-of-shift recall loses a fifth to a third of the minor stops, which is why the practice is a code at the point of occurrence rather than a paragraph at the end of the day.
- **Run the review as a loss drill, not a data download.** Fixed day, capped at thirty minutes, and every agenda item ends with an owner and a due date. The fifteen-minute daily version has a published clock: headline figures, the top losses ranked by minutes, the root cause of exactly one, one action with one owner and one date, then close out yesterday's actions.
- **Verification is a separate step from action, and it is the step everyone skips.** Nothing is closed until the countermeasure is checked — at 30, 60 and 90 days — because problems return when only the action was tracked.
- **These systems fail at the supervisor tier first:** escalation stops and the leader's own routine is abandoned while the boards stay clean and the problems accumulate. A clean board and a rising problem count is the signature.
- **Contrarian, and decisive for any scorecard:** the moment people are judged on a composite number it improves on the report and not on the floor (Goodhart's law); published world-class benchmarks are misapplied to a line with many changeovers a day; and a target set before there is a baseline teaches the team that the number is not honest. Set the target from what the process actually does.

## Meetings, notes and decisions

- **Minutes are a decision record, not a transcript:** attendance, quorum and every decision taken, draft until approved at the next occurrence.
- **The reasoning is what dies, not the action list.** Action items survive because they are already queue-shaped; the reasoning, the constraint raised in passing and the thing you agreed *not* to do stay in a record nobody re-reads. One line of reasoning under each decision — "she leads it; it is a stretch, but she asked for it" — because months later the reasoning matters more than the decision.
- **One action, one named owner, one date.** An action nobody owns is an action nobody does.
- **Log the decision and the hypothesis while the outcome is still unknown**, and revisit the log on a fixed cadence: the review, not the writing, is where the learning is.
- **Open with the previous occurrence's open items, close with the decisions.** Restate what was decided, what is still open and who owns it, then circulate the record.
- **Close the parking lot as part of closing the meeting:** every off-agenda item is extended, scheduled or dropped — a parking lot that is only kept becomes a second agenda nobody visits.
- **A stand-up is where the work attends, not a status round for the manager.** Obstacles are posted publicly and somebody removes them; an obstacle raised and never removed teaches people to stop raising them.
- **Contrarian:** notes nobody reads change nothing — reading is unenforceable while attendance is observable — so put any review where a second person notices whether it happened, and remember that a manager without the authority to decide produces drag that no tracking system fixes.

## The people layer

- **Take notes in the one-on-one, for yourself.** They are the manager's recall, not a document for the report; a short form with a follow-up field, re-read before the next conversation, is the whole mechanism.
- **Keep a running file per person** — dated one-liners of wins, misses and third-party praise — so that a review is not recency-driven.
- **Document observable behaviour with a date, for everyone**, not only for the people in trouble, consistently across the team, and never as a substitute for the official record. The risk in private notes is inconsistency and vendetta, not the notebook; the fix is discipline, not secrecy.
- **Four lines per conversation:** what I owe, what they owe, one dated observation, and the development direction — read back before the next one, re-read in full once a quarter. The test for anything written: would it survive being read out to the person?
- **Ask the person how you are making their job harder**, and close the loop on decisions, not only on tasks: tell the people who contributed what was decided and why.
- **A successor list created once and never examined is not evidence** of anything, and the knowledge that leaves with a person is the tacit part — test it by whether the work stalls while they are away.
- **Contrarian:** a lessons-learned document nobody reads is not institutional memory and a knowledge base nobody consults at the start of new work is just expensive documentation; the documented trap of leader standard work is completing the tasks while changing nothing about how you coach.

## The ways this decays

- Nobody owns the design of the cadence, so it keeps firing and decides nothing.
- Leader standard work is the first thing abandoned, escalation stops, and the boards stay clean while the problems pile up.
- The checkboxes get ticked and the coaching does not change.
- The measures are judged instead of used, so they improve on the report.
- The register or ledger is written once and never reviewed — the failure is cadence, not content.
- Action is taken and never verified, so the same problem returns in a quarter.
- The record is filed where nobody looks at the start of new work, which turns knowledge into cost.

## How to use this page

- Read it before building or changing a routine, not after — and build the smallest version that can be attended to, because every source's failure story is a maintained system that stopped being maintained.
- Take one rhythm, one numbers rule and one people habit, and let them run for weeks before adding the next: what is deliberately refused is part of the design.
- The loop that consumes what these rhythms produce — a commitment, a deviation, an unanswered question, each with an owner and a date — is in [[how-open-work-is-managed]]; the loss vocabulary the numbers are classified with is in [[tpm-pillars-and-losses]]; what the work itself teaches accumulates in [[lessons]].
