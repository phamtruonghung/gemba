# Job Instruction, the four steps

> Distilled from the Art of Lean reference library (artoflean.com): its Job Instruction learning guide — the four-step method and the Job Breakdown Sheet. Job Instruction is the TWI training method Toyota adopted and kept; the guide's own framing is that the four steps are the whole method and everything else serves them. Related: [Standardized work: three elements, three forms](/notes/demo-standardized-work-three-elements-three-forms) · [One-point standard — how to write so others do it right](/notes/demo-one-point-standard-how-to-write-so-others-do-it-right)

## Why telling and showing is not enough

The usual ways of teaching — telling, showing, illustrating, asking questions — are unreliable on their own, and the failure is predictable: the instructor skips exactly the points that make the job work, because they know the job too well to see them. The person then performs the motions and not the job, and the gap surfaces later as a defect, an injury, or a question the worker was afraid to ask.

Job Instruction fixes that with a fixed sequence and one motto worth keeping on the wall: **if the worker hasn't learned, the instructor hasn't taught.**

## The four steps

| Step | What the supervisor does |
|---|---|
| 1 — Prepare the worker | Put them at ease; name the job; find out what they already know; give them a reason to care; place them where they can see the work as it will look when they do it, not mirror-reversed |
| 2 — Present the operation | Tell, show and illustrate one major step at a time; then add the key points; then the reasons. No more than the worker can master in one pass |
| 3 — Try out performance | The worker does the job, four passes, each asking for more |
| 4 — Follow up | Put them on their own; name a specific person other than the supervisor to ask for help; check often at first and taper off |

Two details carry most of the value.

**Presentation is layered, not a single demonstration.** The job is shown three times: the major steps, then the major steps with the key points, then the major steps with the key points and the reasons. What → how → why, each layer added only after the previous one landed. Announce how many major steps there are before starting, so the worker has a map.

**Try-out is the step that separates instruction from showing.** Four passes: do it silently while the supervisor corrects errors on the spot; do it again naming the major steps; again naming the key points; again giving the reasons. Four checkpoints — the motions, the steps, the key points, the reasons. A worker who can do the job and explain why has learned it; one who can only copy the motions has not. The number of passes is a minimum, not a target.

Follow-up is a plan, not a disappearance: frequent checks first, then less often, while staying available. Checking every so often for the first stretch and tapering as the work settles is the pattern; going quiet the moment the lesson ends is abandonment, and it is where hard-won skill quietly erodes.

## The Job Breakdown Sheet, done before the teaching

The four steps alone are not enough. The real work of instruction happens before the worker arrives: taking the job apart. A breakdown has three columns.

| Column | Answers | The test |
|---|---|---|
| Important step | What advances the work | Does this segment carry the job forward, and can it be demonstrated? Reaching for a tool is a motion; inserting the pin into the right hole is a step |
| Key point | What makes or breaks the step | Anything that could ruin the result, hurt someone, or make the work harder than it needs to be |
| Reason | Why the key point matters | What turns a rule the worker obeys into one they understand and keep |

Key points come in three kinds, and the third is the one that hides: a **knack** (feel, timing, a trick of the trade — the thing an experienced person does without thinking and therefore never teaches), a **safety** point (guard, grip, body position, hazard), and an **ease** point (a better grip, a smoother route, a position that saves effort).

Making one is fieldwork, not desk work. Run the job, or watch it done, at the workplace; list the steps in the worker's own words; go back through each step asking what affects quality, safety and ease; write a short reason for each key point that survives. Drawing it from memory or from the specification produces a tidier job than the one on the floor and leaves out exactly the knacks that make the real work go right.

Two limits matter. The breakdown is **the instructor's note to themselves** — brief by design, not a full time-and-motion study. And handing it to the worker is not training: worker-facing instruction sheets and job aids are a different document for a different audience, written after the job has been taught.

## How the pieces fit the floor

- The breakdown is how a standardised work chart becomes a teachable job. Standardized work says what the current best method is; the breakdown says which parts of it a person can get wrong.
- A trained worker is the fastest route to a stable process. Most "operator error" on a line is a job that was never broken down — which is why the blame sentence is nearly always pointed at the wrong end.
- Breakdowns are not only for new people. Any change in method, machine, material or part that matters — a revision, a retooling, a new model — needs the sheets refreshed, or the experienced worker is being asked to relearn silently.

## What to check before calling someone trained

- Can the person name the major steps while doing the work? If not, try-out never got past pass one.
- Can they give the reason for the key points? That is the difference between understanding and imitation.
- Is there a named person, other than the supervisor, they can ask? A worker who guesses instead of asking is a defect waiting for a shift when the supervisor is elsewhere.
- Is the job's breakdown written down and findable? A breakdown that lives in one supervisor's memory leaves with that supervisor.

## Where it does not reach

- Good instruction on a bad method just produces bad work consistently. Fix the standard first, then teach it.
- Job Instruction teaches a job to a person. Handling the person's own problem — attendance, attitude, a grievance, a conflict — is a different method, [Job Relations, the four steps](/notes/demo-job-relations-the-four-steps).
